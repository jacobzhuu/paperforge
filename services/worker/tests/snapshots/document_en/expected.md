# Poisoning attacks on sequential recommenders

## Abstract

Sequential recommender systems model user behavior as ordered sequences of interactions, such as clicks or purchases, to predict the next item a user will engage with. Unlike traditional collaborative filtering, which treats user-item interactions as independent, sequential recommenders explicitly capture temporal dynamics and short-term intent through architectures such as recurrent neural networks, self-attention, and convolutional sequence models. The growing reliance on sequential models in e-commerce, streaming, and social media has raised concerns about their robustness to adversarial manipulation of training data, commonly referred to as poisoning attacks. Poisoning attacks inject carefully crafted fake user profiles or interaction sequences into the training set with the goal of altering the model's recommendations for specific target items or degrading overall performance. This review examines the research question of how poisoning attacks affect sequential recommenders, focusing on the mechanisms by which malicious sequences influence model behavior and the extent to which existing detection methods can mitigate such threats. The scope includes both white-box attacks, where the attacker has full knowledge of the model and training data, and black-box attacks, where only limited information is available, as well as targeted and untargeted attack objectives. The review is organized around three main sections: attack objectives, which describe what attackers aim to achieve; detection limits, which discuss the challenges and current approaches for identifying poisoned data; and a concluding synthesis of open problems.

## Attack Objectives

A small injection budget already moves target rankings in sequential recommenders. [1] The primary objective of poisoning attacks is to promote a chosen item by manipulating the training data that the recommender learns from. [1] Lovelace and colleagues demonstrate that injecting only 0.5% synthetic profiles raises the target item's hit rate from 0.7% to 14.2% on MovieLens-1M. [1] This twenty-fold increase shows that even a minimal injection budget can substantially alter the visibility of a target item. [1] The attack works by crafting user profiles that interact with the target item in a way that biases the sequential model's learned representations. [1] Because sequential recommenders rely on order and co-occurrence patterns, a few strategically placed interactions can have an outsized effect on ranking. [1] The cost to the attacker is low, as the injected profiles need not be realistic or diverse; they only need to align with the model's training objective. [1] Thus, the attack objective is achievable with a minimal budget, making poisoning a practical threat for sequential recommenders. [1]

## Detection Limits

At a 3% false-positive rate, the entropy detector recalls 91% of injected profiles when the injection ratio is sufficiently high. [1] However, below a 0.2% injection ratio, recall collapses to 38%, indicating that the detector fails to identify the majority of attacks. [1] Consequently, attackers can evade detection by spreading their influence across a larger number of minimally altered profiles, staying below the detection threshold. No study has yet compared entropy-based detection with alternative methods on a shared benchmark under low injection ratios, leaving the practical security implications uncertain.
