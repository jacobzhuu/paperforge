# Poisoning attacks on sequential recommenders

## Abstract

Sequential recommenders, which model the order of user interactions to predict the next item of interest, have become a dominant architecture in modern recommendation systems, and their dependence on behavioral training data raises the question of how they are affected by poisoning attacks, in which an adversary injects fake user profiles into the training corpus to manipulate recommendations. This review examines that question by focusing on two aspects of the problem: the objectives that such attacks pursue, and the limits of the detection mechanisms designed to counter them. The scope is deliberately confined to poisoning attacks on sequential recommendation models rather than adversarial perturbations at inference time, so that the analysis addresses the way manipulated training data reshapes the learned sequence model itself. On the side of attack objectives, the primary goal of a poisoning attack on a sequential recommender is to lift a specific target item into the positions where users actually look, and the evidence indicates that this can be achieved with a remarkably small injection budget, meaning that an adversary need only add a small proportion of fake user profiles to shift the target item into top-ranked recommendation slots. This finding is significant because it implies that the effectiveness of an attack is not bounded by the scale of the resources an adversary must commit; the sequential structure of the model, which is what makes it accurate at predicting next-item interests, is precisely what an attacker can exploit to steer recommendations with modest effort. On the side of detection, the review finds that entropy-based detection of poisoning attacks on sequential recommenders appears reliable only above a low injection ratio, and that its recall collapses precisely in the regime where attacks are most effective. The mechanism behind this limit is that entropy-based detectors rely on statistical signals that become distinguishable only when the proportion of fake profiles is large enough, whereas small injection budgets, which are sufficient to achieve the attacker's objective of promoting a target item, leave the training data close enough to its original distribution that the detector fails to raise an alarm. This creates a practical gap: the range of injection budgets in which attacks succeed is also the range in which detection is weakest, so defenders cannot rely on entropy-based screening alone to catch the most dangerous configurations. A limitation of the review is that its conclusions rest on the findings available in the completed body, which addresses attack objectives and the limits of a single family of detection methods; other detection approaches, other recommender architectures, and other adversarial strategies are outside its scope, so the generality of the observed detection limits cannot be assessed here. Nevertheless, the combined picture is coherent: poisoning attacks on sequential recommenders can achieve their objective of promoting a target item with a small injection budget, while entropy-based detection loses reliability exactly at those low injection ratios, and the conclusion that follows is that current detection defenses are structurally misaligned with the regime in which attacks are most effective, motivating detection methods and evaluation protocols that are explicitly tested against small-budget attacks.

## Attack Objectives

The primary objective of a poisoning attack on a sequential recommender is to lift a specific target item into the positions where users actually look, and the evidence shows this can be achieved with a remarkably small injection budget. [1] In the reported setting on MovieLens-1M, injecting synthetic profiles amounting to only 0.5% of the interaction data raised the target item's hit rate from 0.7% to 14.2%, a more than twenty-fold increase in exposure. [1] The mechanism behind this leverage is that each injected profile is a forged user sequence, and sequential models learn from order-aware patterns, so a handful of well-placed fake histories can teach the model to associate the target item with common preceding states. What the attacker buys with that investment is a permanent shift in the learned ranking function rather than a per-request manipulation, since the poisoned profiles alter the model's training data itself. No study has yet established how the required budget varies with catalog size or sequence length, which is the key unanswered aspect for translating this laboratory finding into a realistic threat assessment.

## Detection Limits

Entropy-based detection of poisoning attacks on sequential recommenders appears reliable only above a low injection ratio, and its recall collapses precisely in the regime where attacks are most effective. [1] The detector's strength at moderate budgets is well documented: it recalls 91% of injected profiles while holding the false-positive rate at 3%. [1] That same evaluation, however, shows recall dropping to 38% once the injection budget falls below 0.2% of the training data. [1] The mechanism behind this failure is statistical: entropy-based screening relies on fake profiles producing unusually uniform or low-entropy interaction patterns, and at very small budgets such profiles contribute too little signal to stand out from natural user variance. This creates a dangerous overlap with the attack objectives reviewed earlier, since small injection budgets are precisely the setting in which attackers can lift a target item's hit rate while remaining undetected. [1] The boundary condition is therefore clear: defenders should not extrapolate the 91% recall figure to low-budget attacks, and no study has yet established a detection method whose recall holds in the sub-0.2% regime.
